Lesson 9 of 14

Friends and enemies

Which birds help each other, and where that matters.

Each bird has two friends and two enemies among the other four. Like most things in the system, the lists differ between the bright and the dark half.

Bright half
BirdFriendsEnemies
VulturePeacock, OwlCrow, Cock
OwlVulture, CrowCock, Peacock
CrowCock, OwlVulture, Peacock
CockOwl, PeacockVulture
PeacockVulture, CockOwl, Crow
Dark half
BirdFriendsEnemies
VulturePeacock, CrowOwl, Cock
OwlCrow, CockVulture, Peacock
CrowVulture, OwlPeacock, Cock
CockPeacock, OwlVulture, Crow
PeacockVulture, CockOwl, Crow

Friendship is mutual in almost every case: if the Owl counts the Crow as a friend, the Crow counts the Owl as one too.

The bright-half line for the Cock is misprinted in the best-known English source, and later books repair it in different ways. Where a pair is left without a relation the site shows it as “neutral”. Details are on Variants and sources.

Where the lists are used

1 · The sharing bird of a turn

Lesson 7 showed that each turn is shared with another bird. If that bird is a friend, a favourable turn keeps its full value. If it is an enemy, the turn is marked down a step. This is the main everyday use.

2 · Between two people

When two people deal with each other, tradition also looks at whether their birds are friends. Friendly birds are said to get on easily. Lesson 11 covers two people in full.

A common misreading

“Enemy” describes a relation between two birds in a table, not between two people. It is one input to a grade, and a mild one: an enemy sharer turns a good turn into a medium one, never into a bad one.

In short. Two friends, two enemies, different in each half. They matter for the bird that shares your turn, and when you compare two people.
