Lesson 13 of 14

The wider tradition

What the old books add: beginnings, journeys, houses, directions and more.

The daily method in lessons 1 to 12 is the core of Pañcapakṣī, but the old books apply it to many particular occasions and surround it with further lore. This lesson is a map of that wider tradition: what the texts say, reported plainly, so that you can recognise it when you meet it. None of it changes how a day is read.

Choosing when to begin

The classical texts treat this as the main purpose of the whole system: choosing the moment to start something. Their lists of occasions are long and practical, from starting a job or a course of study to signing an agreement, opening a business or moving into a house.

Their rule is the one you already know, with three refinements:

  • What counts is the moment the action really begins: the signature, the first step out of the door, the first sentence of the conversation. Preparation can happen at any time.
  • Among good moments there is an order. A Ruling turn in a Ruling slot comes first, then an Eating turn in a Ruling slot, then a Ruling turn in an Eating slot, then Eating in Eating.
  • The day and the company matter. A ruling day is preferred, a death day is never used, and a turn shared with a friendly bird is preferred to one shared with an enemy.

One classical remark concerns Walking: it is called indifferent in general, but a little more favourable in the dark half than in the bright.

Journeys

Setting out on a journey has a chapter of its own in the main English source. The moment of leaving is judged by the bird’s main activity:

Setting out while your bird isHow the texts rate it
RulingRated the best time to set out, whichever bird shares the turn.
EatingRated good, and better still when the turn is shared with a friend.
WalkingRated indifferent: much moving about for little result.
SleepingRated poor. The texts advise putting the start off.
DyingRated worst. The texts say not to begin an important journey.

Posts, houses and ceremonies

For taking up a post or an office, and for laying a foundation or entering a new house, the texts ask for a Ruling slot and prefer its earliest turns. An Eating slot is the second choice and is said to give about half as much.

It is worth knowing how this sits beside the ordinary Indian almanac. For a ceremony, families usually fix the day with the almanac’s own rules. Pañcapakṣī does not replace that. Its part is to choose the best minutes within the chosen day, for the person whose action it is.

Other people

Lesson 11 covered the comparison of two birds at one moment. Around it the texts collect advice of an everyday kind: propose a meeting for a time when your own bird is high; work most easily with people whose birds are friends of yours. One source adds that friendly birds make for an easy partnership or marriage.

Bird friendship is one small consideration among many. It is no reason, on its own, to choose or to turn away a person.

Directions and colours

Each bird has a home direction and a colour, different in the two halves (lesson 2). The texts use them when the time cannot be chosen, such as an appointment set by someone else. The advice is to find the bird that is Ruling at that moment and to sit or stand towards its direction; some add wearing the colour of the bird that is strongest at the time.

A seasonal layer

One source adds that certain solar months are unfriendly to certain birds and advises against starting large projects in them. The Crow alone is given no such month. The lists are not found in the other sources, and this site does not reproduce them.

What this course leaves out

Three further parts of the literature are named here so that you know they exist. They are not taught on this site.

  • Answering questions. A reading is taken from the state of the birds at the moment a question is asked, or from a star the questioner names. A related oracle uses sixty birds.
  • Moments that are not chosen. The texts also read the bird’s state at events such as a birth or the start of an illness as a forecast. These readings are fatalistic and often harsh, and they have no place in planning a day. Nothing in Pañcapakṣī is a substitute for proper care.
  • Remedies, diagrams and rites. Some books add mantras, talismans and rituals, including some directed against other people. The same books warn that misuse returns on the user.

In short. The classical use is choosing the moment to begin, with a preferred order among good moments. Around that sit rules for journeys, posts, houses and meetings, the directions and colours, and a body of forecasting and ritual that this course describes but does not teach.
