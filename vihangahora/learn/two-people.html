Lesson 11 of 14

Two people, one moment

Comparing two birds: the day, then strength.

Much of the old literature is about contests and negotiations: two people, each with a bird, meeting at one moment. The same reading you do for yourself can be done for both and compared.

Step 1 · The day outranks the hour

First compare the kind of day each bird is having. A bird on its ruling day or night stands above a bird on an ordinary day, which stands above a bird on its death day. If the two differ here, that settles it and the finer comparison is not needed.

Step 2 · Compare strength

If both birds are on the same kind of day, multiply the strength of each bird’s slot activity by the strength of its turn activity (the ladder from lesson 3):

strength = slot activity × turn activity

A Ruling turn in an Eating slot is 1.0 × 0.8 = 0.8. A Walking turn in a Sleeping slot is 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.24.

The higher number has the better of the moment.

The fuller version: natural strength

The classical texts add one more factor. The birds are not equal by nature: each has a fixed weight, and it is multiplied in as well.

Crow11
Vulture0.750.75
Owl0.50.25

With this factor, a Crow that is merely in a Ruling turn inside a Dying slot (1 × 0.2 × 1.0 = 0.2) still edges out a Peacock at its very best, Ruling in Ruling (0.125 × 1.0 × 1.0 = 0.125).

That is a striking result, and it is why the texts themselves reserve natural strength for serious contests and treat the birds as equals for everyday purposes. The Vihanga Hora app does the same: its Everyday model leaves natural strength out, and a Book model includes it for those who want the classical arithmetic.

If the two are level

When both birds are in the same activity, the old books fall back on tie-breakers that compare the two people directly. They are recorded here as a curiosity of the tradition:

Traditional tie-breakers
Same activityBright halfDark half
Both RulingMore handsome winsYounger wins
Both EatingElder winsElder wins
Both WalkingYounger winsTaller wins
Both SleepingTaller winsShorter wins
Both DyingNobody winsNobody wins

Using this kindly

The contest language is old. A more useful modern reading is cooperative: when two people need to talk something through, look for a time that is good for both birds, or at least bad for neither. For a family or a team, the traditional advice is to avoid times when anyone’s bird is Dying, and to keep clear of everyone’s death days.

In short. Compare the day first, then slot strength times turn strength. Natural strength is a classical extra for serious cases. For ordinary life, look for times that suit both people.

Check yourself

Bird A is on its ruling day. Bird B is on an ordinary day but in a Ruling turn of a Ruling slot. Who has the better of the moment?
